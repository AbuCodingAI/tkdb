# Australian moths can steer by the tilt of Earth's magnetic field when the stars are hidden

Status: Peer-reviewed · Fully AI generated. Sources may be wrong, please [open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/AbuCodingAI/tkdb/issues).

## In Plain English

Bogong moths fly up to 1,000 kilometres at night to the same mountain caves in Australia, and last year scientists showed they can steer by the stars [S1][S3]. A new study finds they also carry a magnetic compass that keeps them on course using Earth's magnetic field alone when the stars are hidden [S1][S4]. Like night-flying songbirds, the moths seem to read the tilt of the field lines rather than which way is north, and the compass stopped working without violet-blue light or with weak radio noise [S1]. That hints at a light-powered sensor that birds and insects may share, which could help explain how many animals migrate [S1][S3]. The biggest catch: the tests used moths tethered in a small lab arena by one research team, and the sensor itself has not been found [S1][S2][S3].

## Key Findings

- **The study.** Hui Chen, Eric Warrant and colleagues at Lund University, the University of Oldenburg, the Australian National University and the University of South Australia report the results in Nature, published open access on 18 November 2026 [S1][S4][S5].
- **A magnetic compass on its own.** Under a projected starry sky with the stars scrambled so they gave no direction, moths caught during migration flew in their seasonal direction using only a natural magnetic field made by coils: roughly south-west in spring (122 moths) and north-north-east in autumn (126 moths), across two springs and two autumns from 2023 to 2025 [S1].
- **They follow the field.** When the field was rotated by 180 degrees, moths turned by 191 degrees in spring (128 moths) and 205 degrees in autumn (132 moths). With the field switched off, they flew in random directions [S1].
- **Tilt, not north.** Flipping the field's polarity (which way it points) left spring moths on their usual heading (78 moths), but inverting only its inclination (the angle the field lines make with the ground) sent them the opposite way (76 moths). The authors call this an inclination compass, the same type known from night-migrating songbirds [S1][S4].
- **Light and radio tests.** With violet-blue light below 500 nanometres removed, moths lost their magnetic heading in spring (106 moths) and autumn (115 moths), though they could still steer by a natural starry sky without that light. Weak broadband radio noise between about 150 kilohertz and 60 megahertz also left them disoriented (52 and 58 moths) [S1][S2].
- **How outside experts rated it.** In the published referee reports, one referee called it "an excellent paper (with one exception)", the exception being that experiments were not run blind; another praised "elegant, sophisticated and carefully controlled experiments" with "very large sample sizes"; a third called it "careful, high-quality" but said no part of it "dramatically changes understanding of magnetoreception" [S2]. Sea-turtle navigation researcher Ken Lohmann, who was not involved, told National Geographic it is "a fascinating finding" [S3].

## How It Works

The team caught migrating moths with a light trap on Mount Selwyn, about 70 kilometres from the alpine caves, and tested them in a laboratory near Adaminaby built without magnetic metals [S1][S4]. Each moth was tethered inside a flight simulator, a drum lined with a cardboard landscape, where it could turn freely in any direction while a sensor logged its heading for five minutes [S1][S6]. Around the drum, a set of electric coils recreated the local magnetic field, which could be rotated, flipped or cancelled [S1].

Earth's field lines point steeply into or out of the ground near the poles and lie flat at the equator, so their tilt tells an animal whether it is heading toward a pole or toward the equator, without needing to know north from south [S1][S5]. Moving south in Australia, the tilt increases; moving north, it decreases [S4][S5]. A compass that reads only the tilt ignores a polarity flip, which is what the moths did [S1].

One leading idea for how animals sense this tilt is a "radical pair" reaction: blue light excites a protein called cryptochrome in the eye, creating a pair of molecules with unpaired electrons whose chemistry is nudged by the magnetic field [S1][S3]. That idea predicts the compass needs blue-range light and can be jammed by weak radio waves, and both predictions held in these moths [S1]. The moths' brains and eyes were not examined, so the sensor is inferred, not seen [S3].

## Implications

- **Impact (medium).** One referee wrote that earlier studies had not clearly shown that a migrating moth can choose and hold its seasonal direction on its magnetic sense alone; this study does, with a compass that behaves like a songbird's [S1][S2]. If birds and moths, whose ancestors split hundreds of millions of years ago, use the same light-driven trick, it may be a widespread solution, and moths are far easier to record from than birds or turtles [S3][S4]. But one referee noted that a magnetic sense in Bogong moths was reported in 2018 and inclination compasses in monarch butterflies, so the paper consolidates earlier ideas more than it overturns them [S2].
- **Confidence (medium).** The paper is peer reviewed, its referee reports are public, and samples of 52 to 132 moths per condition gave strong statistics [S1][S2]. But every experiment comes from the same team, the moths were tethered for five minutes rather than tracked on real migrations, and the light and radio results support a radical-pair sensor only indirectly [S1][S3].
- **Blinding.** The experiments were not done blind. After a referee objected, a co-author who had not run the experiments re-analysed coded data with a newly written script and the authors report the conclusions were unchanged; that referee then strongly supported publication [S2].
- **Conservation.** Bogong moths have been listed as endangered since 2021 after steep declines [S3]. Warrant suggests light pollution and radio noise could disturb the moths' compasses, but that is his view and was not tested in the wild [S3].

## Open Questions

- Where is the magnetic sensor, and is it really cryptochrome? The team plans to record from the moths' eyes and brain to find out [S1][S3].
- Is the role of cryptochrome in insect magnetic sensing settled? Results in fruit flies are disputed, and the paper and a referee note the field is divided [S1][S2].
- How do free-flying moths combine the star and magnetic compasses on a real migration, especially on cloudy nights or during magnetic storms [S1][S6]?
- Do the moths also have a magnetic "map" that tells them where they are, as sea turtles do, or only a compass [S3]?
- Does everyday radio-frequency noise affect wild moths, as it did in the lab [S1][S3]?
