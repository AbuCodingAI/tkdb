# Chemists break used silicone back down into the raw ingredient used to make it

Status: Peer-reviewed · Fully AI generated. Sources may be wrong, please [open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/AbuCodingAI/tkdb/issues).

## In Plain English

Chemists at TU Berlin found a way to take silicone, the stuff in baking moulds and baby bottle teats, apart all the way back to the liquid chemical that silicone factories start from [S1][S3]. Today almost no used silicone is turned back into new silicone at that level, so this points toward real recycling instead of landfill or burning [S1][S3]. The peer-reviewed paper shows it working on store-bought silicone items in the lab [S1][S2]. The biggest catch: it relies on phosgene, a highly toxic gas, it has only been done at gram scale, and nobody outside the team has repeated it yet [S2][S3].

## Key Findings

Silicones are polymers with a backbone of alternating silicon and oxygen atoms. That Si–O–Si backbone is what makes them heat-resistant and durable, and also what makes them hard to break down [S1][S3]. A team led by Martin Oestreich at Technische Universität Berlin, with computational chemists Zheng-Wang Qu and Stefan Grimme at the University of Bonn, published the method in Nature on 28 October 2026 as an accelerated article preview [S1].

- **Back to the starting monomer.** The method converts silicones into dimethyldichlorosilane (Me2SiCl2), the key building block silicone is made from, at 80 °C [S1][S2][S3].
- **Real household items, in the lab.** Besides pure silicone oils and model compounds, the team used post-consumer items bought in supermarkets, such as cupcake moulds, baby bottle teats and lids [S2][S3]. TU Berlin reports yields of up to 99% for some products and 86% for a mixed batch of used silicone [S3].
- **Yields are "nominal".** Because commercial items contain fillers and additives of unknown amount, the authors counted the whole item as pure silicone when calculating yields and measured the product by NMR in the crude mixture, not by isolating it [S2].
- **Gram scale, with a purified product.** In one gram-scale run with silicone oil, the monomer was isolated by distillation in 48% yield and about 93% purity [S2].
- **Reviewers were positive but cautious.** The four anonymous referees called the work original and high quality; two thought it better suited to the more specialised journal Nature Chemistry, and one said the catalyst looked hard to use industrially. At the referee's request, the authors changed "readily scalable" to "readily performed on gram-scale" [S2].

**What is established:** the chemistry passed peer review at Nature, with referees' concerns and the authors' extra experiments published alongside [S1][S2]. All results come from the authors' own lab and have not been independently replicated.

## How It Works

1. **The chlorine source.** The reaction uses triphosgene, a solid that is easier to handle than phosgene gas but breaks down to release phosgene during the reaction [S2][S3]. About 3 equivalents per silicone unit were needed for complete conversion [S2].
2. **The catalyst.** A silylium ion, a positively charged silicon atom paired with a bulky boron-cluster anion, starts the process [S1][S2]. According to the authors' experiments and the Bonn group's computer calculations, it turns phosgene into a chlorocarbonyl cation (COCl+), which is the species that actually cuts the Si–O bond [S1][S2]. The team also made this cation separately and showed it can drive the reaction, though less efficiently [S2].
3. **Oxygen leaves as CO2.** Each cut swaps the oxygen on silicon for chlorine, and the oxygen ends up in carbon dioxide, which the authors confirmed by NMR [S2][S3].
4. **Conditions.** The reaction runs in 1,2-dichlorobenzene at 80 °C, typically for 24 hours [S2][S3]. In extra tests it still worked outside a glovebox (94% from a model compound) and with some water present, and the catalyst kept working over five reuse rounds [S2].

**Compared with the earlier method.** In 2025 a French team (Vu and colleagues, including Monteil and Raynaud) reported in Science a gallium-catalysed route using boron trichloride at 40 °C that also makes chlorosilanes from silicone waste [S4]. The TU Berlin authors say their approach avoids that method's solid boron oxide byproduct and handles ring-shaped siloxanes better [S2]. A referee noted the new work builds on that French method [S2].

## Implications

If it can be scaled safely, the method would let used silicone feed back into the start of the silicone supply chain rather than being burned, landfilled or ground into lower-grade filler [S1][S3]. TU Berlin says a patent application has been filed and the team is working on alternatives to phosgene and on cleaning phosgene traces out of the recovered monomer [S3].

- **Phosgene is the elephant in the room.** It is extremely toxic. The authors argue industry already handles phosgene on a million-tonne scale, but only where it is made, so a recycling plant would have to be built that way [S2].
- **Not yet a process.** The work is described by its authors as a conceptual study; turning the monomer back into new silicone, and full industrial loops, are outside its scope [S2].
- **Cost and chemicals.** A referee questioned the cost and availability of the specialised catalyst and the use of a chlorinated solvent; the authors point to a cheaper anion and an EU assessment of the solvent [S2]. Using triphosgene also wastes chlorine as carbon tetrachloride [S2].
- **Competition.** The 2025 gallium and boron trichloride method works at a lower temperature and its authors also call it scalable [S4], so it is not yet clear which route, if any, industry will choose.

**Impact:** medium. A genuinely new chemical route to full back-to-monomer silicone recycling, but still a lab method with a toxic reagent and a competing approach already published. **Confidence:** medium. Peer-reviewed in Nature with a public review file showing the referees' questions answered by new experiments, but the full paper was not read here (only its abstract, the review file and the press release), and the results have not been independently reproduced. These are separate judgments.

## Open Questions

- Can the process run at kilogram or tonne scale with safe phosgene handling, or with a less toxic chlorine source [S2][S3]?
- How pure is the recovered monomer from real mixed waste, and can it go straight back into making new silicone [S2][S3]?
- How do actual yields look once fillers and additives in real products are accounted for, rather than nominal yields [S2]?
- How does the cost compare with making new monomer the usual way, or with the gallium and boron trichloride route [S2][S4]?
- Will independent labs reproduce the results [S1]?
