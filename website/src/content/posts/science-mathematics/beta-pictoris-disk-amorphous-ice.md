# Webb finds glassy water ice mixed with carbon dioxide in the Beta Pictoris planet-forming disk

Status: Peer-reviewed · Fully AI generated. Sources may be wrong, please [open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/AbuCodingAI/tkdb/issues).

## In Plain English

Beta Pictoris is a young star near us with a huge disk of dust and rocky leftovers around it, where planets are still being built [S1][S3]. Astronomers long expected ice out there, but earlier searches never found it; now a team using the James Webb Space Telescope reports seeing water ice and frozen carbon dioxide in the disk for the first time [S1][S2]. The water ice looks glassy rather than crystal-like and is mixed with carbon dioxide in about the same proportion seen in our own comets, so the team thinks this ice formed very long ago in deep space and is being set free by frequent crashes between icy bodies [S1][S2]. That matters because it hints that the raw ingredients for water-rich planets may be common around young stars [S3]. The catch: this is one team's reading of one set of telescope observations, the ice type and mix come from comparing the light with models and lab samples, and no other group has checked it yet [S2].

## Key Findings

- **First direct ice detection in this disk.** In a paper published in *Science* on 12 November 2026, a team led by Sarah Betti of the Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI) reports absorption features of water ice near 3 µm and of carbon dioxide ice at about 4.26 µm (ordinary ¹²CO₂) and 4.39 µm (the rarer ¹³CO₂) in starlight reflected by the Beta Pictoris debris disk [S1][S2]. The paper notes that no ices had been directly observed in this disk before and that an earlier photometric search found no 3 µm water ice feature [S2].
- **Mostly glassy (amorphous) water ice.** The team sees no sharp "Fresnel peak" near 3.1 µm and no 1.65 µm feature, both signs of crystalline ice, and its models favour amorphous ice. It sets a 3-sigma upper limit of less than 7% crystalline water ice by volume of the ices (less than 4% of total volume) [S1][S2].
- **Carbon dioxide mixed into the water ice.** The CO₂ features are shifted and broadened compared with pure CO₂ ice in the lab, which the authors attribute to CO₂ trapped in water ice [S2]. From the measured columns they derive a CO₂ to H₂O ratio of 1:9.3 ± 0.1, about 11%, within the 2.5 to 30% range reported for Solar System comets and 7 to 20% in younger planet-forming disks [S2][S3]. The best lab match is a 1:10 CO₂ and amorphous water mixture measured at 10 to 50 K, though the authors say other mixtures are possible [S2].
- **Evenly spread ice, read as many collisions.** The water ice feature is roughly uniform across the mapped disk, and spectra from two spots about 100 astronomical units (au) from the star on opposite sides are almost identical [S2]. The authors argue that a single big collision would leave a lopsided pattern, so many smaller collisions between icy bodies are more likely [S1][S2].
- **Consistent with independent gas evidence.** A separate 2014 ALMA study by a different team found about 0.3% of a Moon's mass of carbon monoxide gas in the disk, 30% of it in one clump about 85 au out, and argued the gas must be continually replenished, most likely from icy bodies being destroyed [S5]. That supports the collision picture, but it is not a second detection of the ice itself.

## How It Works

**The target.** Beta Pictoris is about 20 million years old and 19.6 parsecs (about 64 light-years) away; its disk is seen almost edge-on and contains three known planets [S2][S3]. It is a debris disk: the gas-rich phase is over, and the dust is resupplied by collisions among comets and planetesimals, the small building blocks of planets [S1][S3].

**The observation.** Webb's Near Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec) observed the star on 7 December 2023 in integral field mode, which records a spectrum from 0.6 to 5.2 µm for every point in the image, using four mosaic tiles around the star [S2]. A reference star was observed the same way so that the star's glare could be subtracted, leaving the faint light scattered by the disk's dust [S2].

**Reading the ice.** Ice absorbs light at characteristic wavelengths, so the team converted the disk's brightness to reflectance and measured how deep each dip is [S2][S3]. The shape of the water band tells amorphous from crystalline ice, and the exact position and width of the ¹³CO₂ band indicate what the carbon dioxide is mixed with [S2]. Amorphous ice forms when water freezes very cold and fast; to stay amorphous it must remain below about 100 K or be disordered again by radiation or particles from the star [S2][S3].

**Why collisions.** Ultraviolet light from the star should destroy small grains of pure ice quickly, but "dirty" ice mixed with other molecules can survive longer [S2]. The authors propose that ice formed early, possibly in the interstellar medium, was locked inside icy bodies and is released as small dirty grains when they collide at speeds expected to be too low (under about 2 km/s) to crystallise it [S2]. The 2014 ALMA team reached a related conclusion from gas: carbon monoxide in the outer disk should be broken up by ultraviolet light within roughly 120 years, so it must be replenished, likely by icy debris [S5].

## Implications

- **Interstellar ice may survive into planet building.** If the interpretation holds, a young planetary system 64 light-years away appears to contain ice with much the same make-up as comets in our Solar System, suggesting the ingredients for water-rich planets could be common [S2][S3].
- **A tool for comparing disks.** The paper says the 3 µm water ice feature had been seen in only one other debris disk, around HD 181327, where it was interpreted as crystalline ice; Beta Pictoris shows mostly amorphous ice instead [S2]. Comparing disks of different ages could show how ice changes as systems mature [S2].
- **Impact and confidence, separately.** We rate the impact as medium: a first for a well-studied benchmark disk and a useful clue about where planet-building ice comes from, but not a change in how planets are understood to form. We rate confidence in the detection as medium-high (a peer-reviewed paper with clear features seen on both sides of the star) and confidence in the interpretation (interstellar origin, frequent collisions) as medium, because it depends on models, lab comparisons and assumptions about grain sizes and scattering [S1][S2].

## Open Questions

- **Independent checks.** We found no independent re-analysis of the Webb data and no expert commentary outside the author team; the news coverage we read repeats the paper [S3][S4]. The 2014 ALMA result supports the collision picture but measures gas, not ice [S5].
- **How much crystalline ice, and what else?** The authors say they cannot determine the amount of crystalline ice, only set an upper limit, and cannot exclude other molecules in the mixture; solid carbon monoxide ice was not detected, perhaps hidden by carbon monoxide gas emission [S2].
- **Modelling assumptions.** The analysis assumes the disk is optically thin and that grain-size and scattering effects on band shapes are negligible [S2]. Different assumptions could change the inferred ratios.
- **Many collisions or something else?** The uniform spread is argued to require frequent collisions [S1][S2]; it has not been tested against other ways of spreading icy grains, and the observed ice comes from two apertures plus a map of one feature.
- **Access.** The *Science* full text is paywalled beyond the abstract; detailed numbers here come from the authors' arXiv version posted on 13 November 2026, which matches the journal abstract [S1][S2].
