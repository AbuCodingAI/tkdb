# A 3.95-billion-year-old rock from northern China hints that seafloor was being pushed deep into Earth very early

Status: Peer-reviewed · Fully AI generated. Sources may be wrong, please [open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/AbuCodingAI/tkdb/issues).

## In Plain English

Rocks from Earth's first few hundred million years are almost all gone, so every surviving piece is a rare clue about how the planet's crust began [S1]. Geologists in China found a pale, banded rock in Hebei province, and tiny crystals inside it date it to about 3.95 billion years ago, making it the oldest well-dated rock known outside one site in Canada, if the date holds [S1][S4][S5]. Chemical traces in those crystals suggest the rock formed when old seafloor, once soaked by surface water, was pushed far down into the Earth and partly melted [S1][S3]. The team proposes that a short-lived version of subduction, where one slab of crust sinks under another, did the pushing [S1]. The catch: the age and chemistry were measured, but the deep burial and the subduction idea come from computer models of one small outcrop, and no other team has checked it yet [S1][S2][S5].

## Key Findings

- **Peer-reviewed, one team, no outside repeat.** The paper appeared in *Nature* on 11 November 2026 from geologists at the Beijing SHRIMP Center of the Chinese Academy of Geological Sciences, with co-authors at Jilin, Nanjing and Curtin universities; the authors declare no competing interests and have deposited their data tables publicly [S1][S6]. The main text is paywalled; this report relies on the abstract, figure titles, supplementary information, the published referee reports and news coverage [S1][S2][S5].
- **A very old, measured age.** Uranium-lead dating of zircon crystals from three samples gave an age of about 3.95 billion years; SciQuest reports a weighted mean of 3,951 ± 3 million years from 33 analyses, with the rock later reheated about 3.45 billion years ago [S1][S5]. The only other unequivocally documented rocks older than 3.9 billion years are in the Acasta Gneiss Complex, Canada [S1][S2].
- **Chemistry pointing to altered seafloor and an early depleted mantle.** The zircons have slightly heavy oxygen (average δ18O of +6.77 ± 0.23‰) and positive hafnium values (weighted mean εHf(t) of +1.45 ± 0.27) [S1]. The authors read this as magma from basalt that had been altered by cool water near the surface and that came from a mantle already depleted by crust-making in the Hadean, possibly as early as about 4.3 billion years ago [S1][S3][S4]. That timing is model-dependent: about 4.35 billion years in the base case and no later than about 4.2 billion years in the most lenient case, according to SciQuest [S5].
- **Deep melting is a model result.** Thermodynamic and trace-element models suggest the magma formed from 8 to 16% melting of basaltic crust at more than 1.6 gigapascals and 740 to 820 °C, deeper than about 50 kilometres, which implies quick burial along a cool path [S1][S3][S4].
- **Subduction is the authors' proposal.** They write that the burial "was most likely achieved through a transient subduction event" and, after referee pushback, call this the first *rock-based* evidence for such subduction on the early Earth [S1][S2]. Earlier suggestions of early subduction from detrital zircons, structural geology and computer models already exist [S2].
- **Referees pushed back, then agreed.** Referee Martin Guitreau questioned whether the 3.95-billion-year-old zircons might be inherited from an older rock and whether the samples still record their original melt, which the pressure estimate depends on; Elizabeth Bell asked for checks that the ages and hafnium data match [S2]. The authors added arguments and extra modelling, and both referees recommended publication [S2].

**What is established:** a peer-reviewed age of about 3.95 billion years for a gneiss in Hebei and its zircon isotope values, accepted by two named referees [S1][S2]. **What is not:** that subduction caused the burial, how deep it went beyond model estimates, or any independent re-dating of the rock [S1][S2][S5].

## How It Works

1. **Dating with zircon.** Zircon crystals lock in uranium when they grow, and uranium decays to lead at a known rate, so measuring both with an ion microprobe gives the time the magma cooled [S1][S4].
2. **Oxygen as a surface tracer.** Zircon from magma made straight from the mantle has fairly fixed oxygen isotope ratios; heavier oxygen suggests the source rock had reacted with water at low temperature near the surface before it melted [S1][S5].
3. **Hafnium as a mantle-history tracer.** A positive hafnium value means the source came from mantle that had already lost some material to earlier crust, so the mantle had been "depleted" before the rock formed [S1][S4].
4. **Pressure from chemistry.** The rock belongs to the TTG family (tonalite, trondhjemite, granodiorite) that makes up much of the oldest continental crust [S5]. Its trace-element pattern matches melts formed where plagioclase is unstable, at high pressure; the team modelled melting of an Acasta amphibolite, used as a stand-in because so few mafic rocks this old survive, to estimate the conditions [S1][S2]. This contrasts with the roughly 4.0-billion-year-old Idiwhaa unit at Acasta, which formed at low pressure [S1][S7].

## Implications

When plate tectonics began is one of the biggest open questions about the early Earth, and rocks this old are almost the only direct witnesses [S1][S4]. If the interpretation holds, it suggests that at least one patch of seafloor was dragged to mantle depths within about 600 million years of Earth's formation [S5], and that early Earth had more than one way of making crust, since the slightly older Acasta rocks point to a shallow setting [S1][S5]. It does not show that modern-style plate tectonics was running worldwide; the authors describe a brief, local event [S1][S5].

## Open Questions

- Will other laboratories confirm the 3.95-billion-year age and the isotope values on new samples [S2][S5]?
- Do the analysed samples really preserve the original melt, which the pressure estimate depends on [S2]?
- Could another process, such as melting at the base of thick crust or sinking "drips" of dense crust, explain the burial instead of subduction, as the authors argue it cannot [S2][S5]?
- Are there more rocks this old in the Shimen Complex or elsewhere in the North China Craton [S4][S5]?
