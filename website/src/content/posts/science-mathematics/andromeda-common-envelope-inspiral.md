# Astronomers watched a star spiral inside its giant companion in Andromeda for seven years before it erupted

Status: Peer-reviewed · Fully AI generated. Sources may be wrong, please [open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/AbuCodingAI/tkdb/issues).

## In Plain English

Astronomers say they watched a small star sink into the puffed-up outer gas of a giant star in the nearby Andromeda Galaxy [S1][S2]. For about seven years the giant slowly got brighter, which the team explains as drag heating the gas as the smaller star spiralled inward, and in late 2025 the system blew up in a bright flash [S1][S2]. Scientists have long thought this "shared envelope" stage is how many very tight pairs of stars form, but it had never been caught while it was happening [S1][S2]. The team thinks the small star ended up crashing into the giant's core [S1]. The biggest catch: dust still hides what is left, and another research group argues the two stars may have survived as a tight pair [S2][S4].

## Key Findings

- **The event.** AT 2025abao is an eruption in the Andromeda Galaxy (M31) in late 2025 that three separate groups classify as a luminous red nova, a type of outburst linked to stars merging or sharing an envelope, coming from an old, swollen giant known as an asymptotic giant branch (AGB) star [S1][S3][S4].
- **The slow spiral.** In Nature, Karambelkar and colleagues say a seven-year gradual brightening is best explained by a companion of about 0.7 solar masses spiralling inside the convective envelope of a giant of about 7 solar masses, powered by frictional drag [S1]. They call this "one of the first direct observations" of drag energy loss during common envelope evolution [S1]. The companion was never seen directly; its mass was estimated from the amount of dust around the system [S1][S2].
- **The end.** According to the paper, the companion then merged with the giant's core, throwing off roughly the outermost 10% of the envelope at about 10 times the giant's escape speed [S1]. Lead author Karambelkar told Ars Technica the star brightened about 100-fold in 25 days, but said only around 1 percent of the energy released in the final plunge came out as light [S2].
- **An energy-budget number.** The event constrains the fraction of orbital energy available to unbind the envelope, the "alpha" parameter used in models, to roughly 0.01 to 0.4 [S1]. Ars reports the team found the standard formula works as a rule of thumb but misses some of the physics [S2].
- **Independent observations.** A separate team led by Andrea Reguitti and Andrea Pastorello (published in Astronomy & Astrophysics) collected its own light curves and spectra, found the eruption evolved like a classic luminous red nova peaking at g = 15.1 magnitude, and measured an infrared precursor consistent with an M giant or AGB star [S3].
- **A competing reading.** Another group (Chen and colleagues, a preprint its authors say is accepted by The Astrophysical Journal Letters) models the whole event differently: a companion of about 1.4 solar masses that, with the giant's core, survives in an eccentric orbit for at least 400 days after the envelope phase began [S4].

## How It Works

Some pairs of stars orbit far too close together to have formed that way, so their orbits must have shrunk; common envelope evolution is the leading explanation [S1][S2]. When one star ages into a giant it can swell until its outer gas engulfs its partner, and the two then share one loose envelope [S2]. The buried companion feels drag, loses orbital energy and spirals inward; depending on how much gas gets blown away, the pair either ends up in a much tighter orbit or merges [S2].

The team first noticed the giant brightening in 2023, in infrared data from the WINTER telescope at Palomar and NASA's NEOWISE mission [S2][S6]. They then rebuilt its history from about a dozen telescopes and surveys, back to photographic sky plates from 1989 [S2]. The star barely changed until 2018; modelling those quiet years pointed to a giant about 350 times wider than the Sun [S2]. From 2018 to early 2025 it brightened two- to threefold in visible light, and the team says the extra light matches the energy the drag should release, which Karambelkar compares to a spacecraft re-entering the atmosphere [S2].

NASA's SPHEREx telescope caught the giant swollen and wrapped in new dust in mid-2025 [S2]. After the October 2025 outburst, follow-up came from Swift, Gemini North, Keck and, on 24 December 2025, the James Webb Space Telescope, whose mid-infrared data traced the dust and helped measure how much mass was thrown off [S5][S6]. The team argues gas shed during the slow spiral piled up around the stars' orbital plane, so the blast escaped mostly toward the poles, a possible explanation for hourglass-shaped nebulae [S2].

## Implications

- **Impact (high for the field).** Common envelope evolution is thought to make many of the tightest binaries, including systems that later become gravitational-wave sources, but its physics rests mostly on theory and simulations [S1][S5]. A real event followed from years before the outburst through the eruption gives modellers a measured energy budget to test against [S1][S2].
- **Confidence (medium).** The study is peer reviewed and the eruption itself is independently confirmed [S1][S3]. But the key interpretation, a companion of a certain mass spiralling inside the envelope and then merging, depends on modelling choices, and an independent group fits the same event with a heavier companion that survives [S1][S4]. NASA's release says Webb data "proved" a merger; the lead author himself told Ars that years of further monitoring are needed to tell conclusively [S2][S5].
- **More to come.** The team expects the Vera Rubin Observatory and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope to find several such events a year in nearby galaxies, which would show whether this one is typical [S2][S6].

## Open Questions

- Did the companion merge with the giant's core or survive in a tight orbit, as one competing model suggests? The team plans to watch for years as the dust clears [S2][S4].
- How massive was the companion really: about 0.7 solar masses, as the Nature team infers from the dust, or about 1.4, as the competing model finds [S1][S4]?
- Do other common envelope events, with different stars, give the same energy-budget number [S1][S2]?
- Is this the first such inspiral ever seen, or will earlier events turn out to have shown it too? The paper itself says "one of the first" [S1].
