# Metamaterials designed like networks spread out damage instead of collapsing in bands

Status: Peer-reviewed · Fully AI generated. Sources may be wrong, please [open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/AbuCodingAI/tkdb/issues).

## In Plain English

Engineers usually build "metamaterials", materials whose strength comes from their inner pattern, by repeating one small shape over and over like tiles [S1][S3]. A team from MIT and Northeastern University instead grew patterns more like a leaf's veins or a social network, with beams of wildly different lengths and thicknesses [S1][S3]. In their tests, 3D-printed samples made this way spread damage evenly instead of collapsing layer by layer, and handled impacts better than the regular patterns they compared [S1]. An outside researcher writing in the same journal calls the materials remarkable [S2]. The catch: all the tests come from the team's own lab, on a small number of mostly flat, credit-card-sized plastic samples, and nobody else has repeated them yet [S1][S3].

## Key Findings

- **Peer-reviewed and open access, with outside commentary.** *Nature* published the study on 11 November 2026 (received 12 December 2025, accepted 21 September 2026) from Northeastern University, MIT and Utrecht University, with Carlos Portela and Albert-László Barabási among the authors [S1]. An earlier version appeared on arXiv on 22 November 2025 [S4]. A News & Views by Marco Paggi of the IMT School for Advanced Studies Lucca, who is not an author, accompanied it [S2].
- **Two numbers control the whole design.** Each structure is grown by adding straight beams at random points and angles, each new beam thinner than the last by a set rule, and stopping where it hits another beam [S1]. Only two parameters, the starting thickness and how fast thickness shrinks, set the result, and beam thickness, beam length and number of connections all follow power-law spreads spanning orders of magnitude [S1].
- **A much wider range of stiffness directionality.** Across 2,600 simulated designs, the scale-rich structures covered 77% of the possible combinations of density and anisotropy (how much stiffer a material is in one direction than another), and more than 96% with guided growth, while square, hexagonal and Voronoi patterns covered narrow bands [S1]. The paper describes this as a 190-fold tunable range of elastic anisotropy [S1]. Compression tests on printed samples matched the simulated trend [S1].
- **Damage spreads out instead of forming bands.** In compression tests on samples at about 42% density, square and hexagonal lattices buckled in bands and a Voronoi pattern compacted near one end, while scale-rich samples did not form compaction bands; their localization measure stayed at or below 0.2 up to 25% strain, 37% lower than the Voronoi pattern [S1]. Their energy absorption matched or beat the three reference patterns [S1].
- **Impacts.** In drop-impact tests at 40% density, the scale-rich design showed the highest energy dissipation per unit mass at the lowest peak force among all the reference patterns the team tested [S1].
- **Other demonstrations, mostly in simulation.** Simulations of a pattern designed to shield a fragile plastic disc showed up to 4.9 times lower stress on the disc than reference lattices, with photoelastic images of printed samples pointing the same way [S1]. A lens that focuses vibrations was demonstrated only in computer simulation [S1].

**What is established:** a peer-reviewed design method with simulations and the authors' own lab tests, and an independent expert commentary that describes the mechanical properties as remarkable [S1][S2]. **What is not:** any independent repeat, tests on real products such as helmets, or a demonstration that each property is unique; the authors themselves write that the individual properties "are not independently unique" to these materials and that the advance is getting them together [S1].

## How It Works

1. **Ordinary metamaterials repeat one cell.** Lattices such as square grids and honeycombs copy a single building block many times, so every part of the material has the same size of features [S1][S3]. When one region starts to buckle, its identical neighbours tend to fail the same way, producing bands of collapse [S1][S3].
2. **Borrowing from network science.** Real networks, from the internet to the brain, mix a few hugely connected parts with many small ones [S1][S2]. The team copied that unevenness in physical form [S1].
3. **Growing the pattern.** A program drops a random point, draws a beam through it at a random angle until it meets another beam or the edge, then repeats with ever thinner beams [S1][S3]. Long early beams pick up more crossings, which the authors relate to "preferential attachment", the rich-get-richer rule known from network science [S1].
4. **Why damage spreads out.** Because neighbouring beams have very different lengths and thicknesses, they buckle under very different loads, so one beam failing does not set off a chain reaction across the sample [S1].
5. **Making samples.** The team 3D-printed centimetre-scale polymer samples with features down to about 100 micrometres, and smaller samples by two-photon lithography [S1]. MIT says each tested sample was about the size of a credit card, with beams from microns to centimetres [S3].

## Implications

The authors and MIT point to helmets, packaging, armour and devices that steer or focus vibrations as possible uses [S1][S3]. Paggi's commentary frames the work as bringing ideas from network science into materials engineering [S2], and the authors argue that materials also open new questions for network science, because beam length and thickness matter in a material but not in an abstract network [S1]. These are prospects, not demonstrations: the tests used polymer samples in the lab, the three-dimensional versions were shown only as generated designs and simulations, and no product has been built [S1].

## Open Questions

- Will another lab, printing its own samples, see the same spread-out damage and impact performance [S1]?
- How do the materials behave when made from metals or other materials at full product scale, and in three dimensions rather than flat samples [S1]?
- How much do results vary from one random realization to the next, given the small number of specimens tested in the compression experiments [S1]?
- Can the vibration-focusing lens, so far shown only in simulation, work in a physical sample [S1]?
