# Bendy rubber blades could let tidal turbines drop their pitch machinery, model-scale tank tests suggest

Status: Peer-reviewed · Fully AI generated. Sources may be wrong, please [open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/AbuCodingAI/tkdb/issues).

## In Plain English

Underwater turbines that make electricity from tides get battered by turbulent currents, so big ones use motors and bearings to twist their blades out of strong flows, and that machinery is costly and breaks down [S1][S2]. Engineers in Edinburgh and at the tyre maker Michelin built blades from rubber around a steel core that simply bend when the current gets strong, letting off the extra force by themselves [S1][S2]. In a large test tank, a small model turbine with these blades made as much power as one with stiff blades and cut the fast, wearing swings in force by up to about 28% [S1]. A computer simulation suggested it could match a turbine with the twisting machinery over a whole tide [S1]. The catch: this was a 1.26-metre model, the comparison with real twisting-blade turbines and the full-size benefits are only simulated, and Michelin, which helped do the work, has filed a patent [S1][S2][S6].

## Key Findings

- **Peer-reviewed, one team, with an industry interest.** The paper appeared in *Nature* on 11 November 2026 from the University of Edinburgh and Michelin [S1][S2]. Three authors are Michelin employees, Michelin funded the work and has filed a patent on the technology [S1]. The test data and design code are public [S1][S4].
- **Same power at the design point.** On a 1.26-metre three-bladed model turbine in the 25-metre FloWave tank, which recreates turbulent tidal flows [S2][S3], the flexible blades reached about the same peak power coefficient (about 0.37) as rigid steel blades at rated flow speed [S1][S2]. When the turbine spun faster to cap power in stronger flow, the flexible blades' thrust fell while the rigid blades' rose [S1].
- **Smaller fast load swings.** At rated speed the flexible blades cut fluctuations in power, thrust and blade-root bending moment by about 18%, 23% and 28% compared with the rigid blades [S1][S6]; the university release rounds this to "up to 29 per cent" and the abstract to "up to one-third" [S1][S2]. At the slowest speed tested, fluctuations went up, although from a small base [S1].
- **The pitch-control comparison is a simulation.** Using the measured coefficients in a model of a full tidal cycle, the rubber-bladed turbine with only speed control produced essentially the same energy as a modelled turbine with active pitch control and reached a similar peak thrust, while a rigid blade with speed control alone peaked at about 1.65 times rated thrust [S1]. No real pitch-controlled turbine was tested alongside [S1], and one referee questioned whether the flexible blade's peak thrust was really no higher [S6].
- **Mechanism fits earlier, independent work.** Camera measurements showed the trailing edge lifting and the blade flattening as flow increased [S1]. A Paris group had earlier shown in experiments that elastic wind-turbine blades which bend under load widen a turbine's working range [S5][S7]; that supports the idea in air, not the tidal numbers.

**What is established:** a peer-reviewed, same-tank comparison showing that tuned flexible blades held power and reduced fast load swings against a rigid blade at model scale [S1][S6]. **What is not:** a test against a real pitch-controlled turbine, results at full size or at sea, or proof of the cost savings [S1][S2][S6].

## How It Works

1. **The problem.** Tidal currents are predictable, but turbulence, waves and shear make the force on turbine blades jump around, which wears out parts; large turbines twist ("pitch") their blades with motors to shed load, and such systems are a common source of failures [S1][S2].
2. **The blade.** Each blade has a stiff steel spar near the front, where the blade is thickest, and rubber moulded around it; the back edge is flexible from root to tip [S1][S2]. Rubber is about as dense as water, so the blades can be made neutrally buoyant [S1][S2].
3. **Bending to shed load.** The blade is moulded with extra curve. As the flow strengthens, water pressure bends the trailing edge up and flattens the curve, which lowers the force the blade generates, a bit like a tree bending in wind [S1][S2]. The authors tuned stiffness carefully: too stiff and loads stay high, too soft and the blade loses power [S1].
4. **Scaling.** The authors argue the behaviour depends mainly on the Reynolds number and the Cauchy number (flow force versus material stiffness), so a stiffer rubber could give the same bending at full size; their simulations of a 20-metre turbine gave similar power and thrust curves [S1][S7].

## Implications

If it scales, getting rid of pitch motors, hinges and bearings could make tidal turbines simpler, lighter and cheaper to maintain, and might open turbulent sites now seen as too punishing [S1][S2]. That matters because tidal power is far more predictable than wind or solar, and the UK targets 1 gigawatt of tidal stream capacity by 2035 [S1][S2]. But the university itself says the work is at research stage and needs further development and larger tests before any commercial use [S2], and the cost savings were not quantified [S6].

## Open Questions

- Will the gains hold on a full-size turbine at sea, where blades are composite and 5 to 15 metres long rather than a 0.5-metre rubber casting [S6][S1]?
- How will the rubber trailing edge stand up to millions of bending cycles, given the authors' fatigue estimates are preliminary [S1][S6]?
- How large is the advantage against real composite blades, which already flex a little, instead of the very rigid steel baseline used here [S6]?
- How much would levelised electricity costs actually fall [S1][S6]?
