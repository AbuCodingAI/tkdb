# Stanford's 6-nanometre chip wires conduct better as they shrink, but outside physicists question why

Status: Peer-reviewed · Fully AI generated. Sources may be wrong, please [open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/AbuCodingAI/tkdb/issues).

## In Plain English

The copper wires inside computer chips work worse as they get thinner, which wastes energy and slows chips down [S4][S5]. A Stanford-led team made wires from a different material, niobium phosphide, about 6 nanometres wide, and found they carried current more easily the thinner they got [S1][S5]. If that holds up, it could help chipmakers keep shrinking chips, and the wires were made with methods factories already use [S1][S4]. The biggest catch: the best number depends on subtracting a thin metal base layer from the measurement, outside experts doubt the team's explanation, and the wires have only been tested in a lab, not inside real chips [S2][S3][S4].

## Key Findings

- **The study.** Haotian Su, Eric Pop and colleagues at Stanford University, SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory, the Weizmann Institute of Science, Penn State and the Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) report the work in Nature, published on 25 November 2026 after review that began in January [S1][S5].
- **Tiny ribbons.** The team patterned flat ribbons of niobium phosphide (NbP) as narrow as about 5 to 6 nanometres and about 1.5 nanometres thick, using top-down methods the authors call industry-friendly [S1][S5].
- **Thinner is better.** The NbP ribbons' resistivity (how strongly a material resists current, regardless of its size) went down as the ribbons got narrower, the opposite of copper [S1][S4].
- **The headline number.** At about 9 square nanometres of cross-section, the authors report an NbP resistivity of 18 ± 4.1 micro-ohm centimetres at room temperature, which they say is lower than conventional metals and semimetals of similar size [S1][S6]. That value is extracted: the NbP is grown on a 3.4-nanometre niobium base ("seed") layer, and the team subtracts that layer's conduction, separately measured on control samples [S3]. A referee noted that the measured two-layer stack itself performs about as well as ruthenium, a metal already being developed to replace copper, at the smallest sizes [S2].
- **Doubts about why.** Judy Cha of Cornell called making the ribbons a "superimportant step forward", but she and Ching-Tzu Chen of IBM, both not involved, question whether the effect really comes from the material's "topological" surface states or is affected by the niobium layer [S4]. Nature's referees raised the same concerns, and also asked whether the NbP is amorphous or nanocrystalline [S2][S4].
- **Peer review outcome.** After three rounds and added experiments, all three referees supported publication; one wrote that "some aspects of the theoretical interpretation remain incomplete" [S2].

## How It Works

A chip can hold some 100 kilometres of copper wiring linking its transistors, and the narrowest wires in leading chips are about 15 nanometres wide [S4][S5]. At that scale, electrons in copper bounce off the wire's surfaces and the boundaries between its tiny crystals, so resistance climbs; Science reports that wire delays can be up to 20 times longer than the time a transistor takes to switch [S1][S4].

NbP belongs to a class called topological semimetals. In these materials, some current can flow along special states at the surfaces and edges, where electrons are less likely to bounce backwards, a property the team calls "spin-momentum locking" [S4][S5]. As a ribbon gets thinner, its surfaces make up a bigger share of it, so if those surface states carry current well, the whole ribbon should resist current less [S4]. Pop compares copper to a pipe narrowed to a straw, whose walls start to impede the flow, while NbP's walls are "electrically more slippery" [S5].

The team sputtered NbP films at 400 °C onto a niobium seed layer on sapphire, capped them, and cut them into ribbons of different widths, then measured their resistance and treated the NbP and niobium as two parallel conductors to work out the NbP's share [S3]. The effect showed up only in the thinnest films: in 1.5- and 5-nanometre-thick NbP the extracted resistivity fell as ribbons narrowed, while 10-nanometre-thick NbP behaved like an ordinary metal and got worse below about 30 nanometres wide [S3]. The longest ribbons survived about 340 million amperes per square centimetre before failing; the authors estimate about 110 million on the insulating materials used in real chips [S3].

## Implications

- **Impact (medium).** Wiring resistance is a real obstacle to shrinking chips, and IBM, Samsung and Cornell groups are also testing topological conductors [S4]. A material that gets better as it shrinks, made with factory-style methods and with TSMC researchers as co-authors, is a credible step toward a copper replacement [S1][S5]. But the measured stack only matches ruthenium so far, and there is no data yet on long-term reliability or building these wires into a working chip [S2][S5].
- **Confidence (medium).** The paper is peer reviewed and its referee reports are public, and the final round of review judged the error analysis good enough to claim the shrinking-resistance trend "with good confidence" [S2]. But the headline number depends on a model that subtracts the niobium layer, outside researchers question the explanation, and no other group has repeated the result [S2][S4].
- **The explanation is open.** Pop acknowledges other explanations are possible, such as charge building up at the surface for reasons unrelated to topology, while saying the results are consistent with a topological conductor [S4]. The practical trend and the physics behind it should be judged separately.
- **Not ready for chips.** Stanford says the team still needs to study endurance, fabrication compatibility and behaviour at different temperatures; Pop said, "We didn't come up with a better copper right away" [S5][S6].

## Open Questions

- Would NbP ribbons keep their low resistivity without the niobium seed layer, or with a seed layer that adds less conduction [S2][S4]?
- Is the trend really caused by topological surface states, and is the NbP crystalline enough for those states to exist [S2][S4]?
- Can another laboratory reproduce the shrinking resistance with its own NbP ribbons [S4]?
- How do the wires hold up over years of current and heat on a real chip dielectric rather than sapphire [S3][S5]?
- Will NbP beat ruthenium and other copper alternatives once whole wires, not extracted values, are compared [S2]?
