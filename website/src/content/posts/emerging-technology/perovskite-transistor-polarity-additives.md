# Screening 100 million molecules finds additives that steady fast perovskite transistors

Status: Peer-reviewed · Fully AI generated. Sources may be wrong, please [open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/AbuCodingAI/tkdb/issues).

## In Plain English

Perovskites are cheap crystals that can be made into transistors, the tiny switches inside electronics, but charged atoms inside them tend to drift and make the switches unreliable [S1]. A team led from Jilin University in China used computers to sort through more than 100 million possible additive molecules and pick ones that should grip those drifting atoms [S1][S3]. They report that their best additive gave transistors that carried current well and kept switching steadily for more than 9,000 on-off cycles [S1]. Outside researchers writing in the same journal say the idea of using a molecule's polarity as a guide is useful for designing these materials [S2]. The big catch: this is a small lab result that no one has repeated yet, and another group has already reported faster tin perovskite transistors by a different method [S5].

## Key Findings

- **Peer-reviewed, with outside commentary.** *Nature Electronics* published the study on 11 November 2026 (received 10 December 2025, accepted 7 October 2026), alongside a News & Views by Tian Tian, Suidong Wang, Junhao Chu and Wenwu Li of Fudan University and the Macau University of Science and Technology, who are not authors of the paper [S1][S2]. The paper states that Wenwu Li was also one of its peer reviewers [S1].
- **A polarity-guided search.** The team screened fluorinated ammonium-based molecules able to form two hydrogen bonds, across a chemical space of more than 10^8 molecules, and identified 14 candidates combining high molecular polarity with a suitable size [S1][S3]. The deposited data description adds that the shortlist was also chosen to avoid forming two-dimensional perovskite layers [S3].
- **Three additives worked well in devices.** Transistors made with three of the additives had field-effect mobilities above 25 cm²/V·s [S1].
- **Best result.** The best additive, 4-(trifluoromethyl)-phenethylammonium (CF3PEA), gave a mobility of 41.3 cm²/V·s, an on/off current ratio of about 10^7 and stable switching currents over 9,000 cycles [S1][S3].
- **The authors' explanation.** The paper's figures report how polarity affects film crystallinity and device performance, how the additives interact with formamidinium, iodide and tin ions in FASnI3 films, and how devices with different additives compare in electrical stability [S1]. TKDB could read only the abstract, figure titles and data description, not the full text, so the detailed evidence for this mechanism was not checked [S1].

**What is established:** a peer-reviewed lab demonstration, with an independent commentary agreeing that molecular polarity can be used to pick useful additives [S1][S2]. **What is not:** any independent repeat of the device results, or a record mobility; a Fudan-led group reported tin perovskite transistors averaging above 60 cm²/V·s in 2025 using a different additive [S5].

## How It Works

1. **The problem: drifting ions.** In a perovskite transistor the gate applies a strong electric field, which pushes on charged ions in the crystal. When those ions move, the device shows hysteresis (its response depends on its recent history) and becomes unstable [S1]. The same Jilin group had earlier reported that migrating formamidinium ions in tin perovskite transistors cause hysteresis and instability, and that hydrogen bonding with fluoride can restrain them [S4].
2. **Additives as anchors.** The idea is to add a small amount of an organic molecule that binds firmly to the mobile ions and holds them in place [S1][S2].
3. **Polarity as the selection rule.** A highly polar molecule has a strongly uneven spread of charge, which the authors propose strengthens its grip on mobile ions [S1][S2]. Rather than testing additives one by one, the team ranked a huge set of candidate molecules by computer, using polarity and size, then made devices with the most promising ones [S1][S3].
4. **The device.** The transistors use a tin-based perovskite, formamidinium tin iodide (FASnI3), as the channel that carries current [S1].

## Implications

Perovskite transistors have reached mobilities comparable with commercial polycrystalline silicon devices, but poor stability and hysteresis have kept them out of real products [S1]. A rule of thumb that predicts which additives will suppress ion drift could save trial-and-error work: the News & Views authors call molecular polarity a predictive descriptor for screening additives, and the authors say their strategy can be extended to other perovskite systems [S2][S3]. Tin-based perovskites are also of interest as p-type transistor materials, which are harder to make well than n-type oxide transistors [S5]. These are prospects, not demonstrated uses: the results come from lab devices, and the sources fetched do not report circuits, large-area manufacturing or long-term operation in air [S1][S3].

## Open Questions

- Will another lab reproduce the 41.3 cm²/V·s mobility and 9,000-cycle stability with the same additive [S1]?
- How well do the computer's polarity rankings predict device performance across all 14 shortlisted molecules, not just the best three [S1][S3]?
- How do the devices hold up in air, under long bias stress, or in circuits, conditions the abstract does not cover [S1]?
- Can polarity-guided additives be combined with other approaches, such as the one behind the higher mobilities reported by the Fudan-led group [S5]?
